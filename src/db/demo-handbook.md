Atmosphere Press Author Handbook (Demo Edition)

This is a short, fixture version of the real Author Handbook, written so the portal's assistant
has something grounded to answer questions from in demo mode. It covers the shape of the
Atmosphere production process in six stages, plus how payment works.

1. Onboarding

Onboarding begins the moment your manuscript is accepted and your Author Agreement is signed.
Within the first two weeks, your Book Production Manager (BPM) reaches out to introduce
themselves and confirm the production package you selected — Essential, Plus, or Premium. Your
Publishing Coordinator (PBC) is assigned at the same time and becomes your day-to-day point of
contact for scheduling and logistics questions. Your Acquisitions Editor (AE) stays attached to
the project as the person who first said yes to your book and can speak to its overall vision.

During onboarding we collect your contact details, confirm your manuscript file is production-
ready, and set an initiation date, which is the official start of your production timeline. We
also send a production calendar with rough windows for each stage — developmental editing,
proofreading, cover design, interior design, and publicity — though the pipeline is not strictly
linear. Books sometimes move between stages, run two stages in parallel, or repeat a stage after
feedback. The portal's timeline view reflects this: it shows dated milestones as they happen
rather than a single fixed bar. If two weeks pass with no contact from your BPM, reach out to
your PBC directly.

Most onboarding questions are logistical rather than editorial: confirming your legal name for
copyright registration, your preferred author bio, and whether you're publishing under a pen
name. We also ask for a short list of comparable titles, which helps your cover designer and
publicity team position your book later on. None of this needs to be perfect on day one — you can
revise your bio, comps, and even your title up until interior design begins.

2. Developmental Editing

Developmental editing is the deepest pass your manuscript receives. Your Developmental Editor
(DE) reads for structure, pacing, character arcs, plot logic, and thematic clarity — the big-
picture elements that are hardest to see from inside a manuscript. This is not a line edit; a DE
will not fix your commas, but they will tell you if your third act rushes, if a character's
motivation is unclear, or if two subplots are competing for the same emotional space.

Once your DE is assigned, the portal shows their name and an assignment date, and a status of
"in progress" while they work. Expect a developmental edit letter (several pages of prose notes)
plus an annotated manuscript within four to ten weeks, depending on length and your package tier.
After you receive the letter, you'll have a revision window — typically three to six weeks — to
work through the notes at your own pace. You are not obligated to accept every suggestion; a
developmental edit is a conversation, not a mandate. When your revisions are in and your DE has
signed off, the status flips to "complete" and your book moves to proofreading.

It's normal to feel defensive about a first read of developmental notes — most authors do. Give
yourself a few days before responding, and remember that a DE's job is to represent a first-time
reader's experience of your book, not to impose their own story on top of yours. If a note feels
wrong, it's worth asking your DE to explain the problem they're seeing rather than the fix they
suggested; often there's a different, better solution once you understand what isn't landing.

3. Proofreading

Proofreading is a close, line-level pass for grammar, spelling, punctuation, and internal
consistency — character names that drift, timeline errors, repeated words, formatting slips. Your
Proofreader (PR) works from your revised, post-developmental-edit manuscript, so it's important
that developmental revisions are fully folded in before this stage starts.

Some books also go through an optional cold read: a fresh reader with no prior context reviews
the manuscript specifically to catch anything earlier passes missed, since familiarity with a
text makes errors easier to skip over. Not every book needs a cold read; your BPM will tell you if
yours does. Proofreading marks the last stage where substantial text changes are expected. Once a
manuscript clears proofreading, it is considered locked for interior layout, so this is your last
practical chance to catch phrasing you're unhappy with — not just typos.

Your Proofreader works in tracked changes or margin comments, depending on your package, and you
will get a chance to review and accept or query every suggestion before the file is considered
final. Because proofreading is the gate before layout, this stage tends to have the firmest
deadlines in your production calendar — if you need more time to review the marked-up manuscript,
tell your PBC as early as possible so the interior design slot can be adjusted.

4. Cover Design

Cover design usually starts once your manuscript is through developmental editing, so your Cover
Designer (CD) has a settled sense of tone, genre, and content before sketching concepts. Your CD
will ask you to fill out a design brief covering comparable titles, visual preferences, must-avoid
imagery, and any back-cover copy you've drafted.

You'll typically see two to three initial concepts, each with a brief rationale for the visual
choices. Feedback rounds are iterative — most books land on a direction within two or three
rounds. When you're happy with a concept, you'll formally approve it, and the portal records this
as "Approval Received." A cover cannot go to print without your explicit written approval; this
is one of the few points in production that always waits on you specifically, so try not to let a
cover concept sit unanswered for more than a week or two.

Cover approval also unblocks other work: your ISBN and metadata get finalized around the same
time, and the cover file itself is what publicity uses for retailer listings and promotional
graphics later on. If you're torn between two directions, ask your CD which one they'd recommend
for your genre's shelf — designers who work across many Atmosphere titles have a good feel for
what stands out in a crowded category without looking like it's trying too hard.

5. Interior Design

Interior design is where your locked manuscript becomes an actual laid-out book: trim size,
typeface, chapter openers, running heads, and page numbers all get set by your Interior Designer
(ID). This stage cannot begin until proofreading is fully complete, because any late text change
would ripple through pagination.

You'll receive a formatted PDF proof to review — check that chapter breaks land where you expect,
that any images or drop caps look right, and that nothing was dropped in the conversion from
manuscript to typeset pages. This is a layout review, not another editing pass; please resist the
urge to rewrite sentences here; substantial text changes at this stage can delay your schedule and
may incur a fee. After your proof approval, a short interior proofing pass double-checks the
final, paginated file before it's locked for print and ebook conversion.

Ebook and print files are generated from the same locked interior, so a single approval covers
both formats — you won't need to review a separate ebook layout unless you've specifically
requested enhanced formatting (embedded images in a memoir, for instance). Once your interior
proof is approved, your file is essentially frozen; any change from here forward goes through a
formal post-lock correction request rather than the normal proofing workflow.

6. Publicity

Publicity typically begins six to eight weeks before your publication date, once your cover and
interior are both locked. Our publicity team prepares your Amazon and retailer listings, an author
page, review-copy outreach to trade and genre reviewers, and a launch plan tailored to your goals
— whether that's a local event, a blog tour, or a quiet, steady release.

This is the stage where your own outreach matters most: personal invitations to your existing
readers, a mailing list announcement, or local media contacts you may have will meaningfully
extend what our publicity team can do centrally. We'll share a publicity questionnaire early in
this stage to gather your existing platform, any planned events, and people you'd like sent an
advance copy. Publication day itself is a milestone on your portal timeline, and the book's status
moves to "Published" once retailer listings go live.

Publicity is also the stage most affected by your own participation, so it's worth starting to
think about it well before your questionnaire arrives. A short list of podcasts, newsletters, or
local bookstores you'd like approached, even without contacts already in place, gives our team a
concrete starting point. Reviews from trade outlets can take six to eight weeks to come back after
a copy is sent, which is part of why this stage starts as early as it does relative to your
publication date.

7. Payment Schedule

Production is paid in installments tied to your package, not to individual stages. Most authors
pay a deposit at signing, a second installment around the start of developmental editing, and a
final installment before interior design begins; the exact split depends on your package tier and
is spelled out in your Author Agreement. If an installment is due, you'll see an action item on
your dashboard with a "Pay now" link — production on a paid stage is never interrupted for a
payment already received, but the next stage generally will not start until an outstanding
installment clears. If you have questions about your specific schedule or need to arrange a
different cadence, contact your Publishing Coordinator (PBC); payment logistics are one thing your
BPM will always defer to your PBC on.
